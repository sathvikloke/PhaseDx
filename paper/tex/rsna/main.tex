{\large\bfseries What a Slice-Level Benchmark Certifies without the Pixels:
An Audit of Seven Public Imaging Datasets\par}

\vspace{0.8em}

\textbf{Article Type:} Original Research

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Summary Statement}

\textbf{A model that never sees a pixel reached about half of the published
margin over chance on public volumetric imaging benchmarks read slice by slice,
and on the largest benchmark the same scores fell below chance when read one
patient at a time.}

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Key Points}

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0.1em}
\item On \num{18938} patients, one pixel-blind score vector read 0.737 (95\%
confidence interval: 0.735, 0.740) per slice and 0.453 (95\% confidence
interval: 0.445, 0.461) per patient; nothing changed between the two readings
except the unit of evaluation.
\item Against peer-reviewed comparators, a model with no access to the images
reached a median 0.469 (interquartile range: 0.437, 0.490; nine
benchmark-arms) of the published margin over chance.
\item On one of those nine benchmark-arms the same model reached none of the
published margin ($-0.002$), so a benchmark can register as clean on this
measure.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Abbreviations}

AUC = area under the receiver operating characteristic curve;
CI = confidence interval;
DWI = diffusion-weighted imaging;
ICH = intracranial hemorrhage;
IQR = interquartile range.

\vspace{0.4em}

\textbf{Keywords:} CT; MRI; Computer Applications---Detection/Diagnosis;
Technology Assessment; Brain/Brain Stem.

\section*{Abstract}

\textbf{Purpose:} To quantify how much published slice-level performance on
public volumetric imaging benchmarks is reachable from the benchmark's own
label file with no images, and what those scores show per patient.

\textbf{Materials and Methods:} Retrospective secondary analysis of
de-identified public label files from seven imaging benchmarks released
2016--2024, including NYU fastMRI; analyses were run July 2026. The largest,
the 2019 RSNA Intracranial Hemorrhage benchmark, held \num{752802} slices from
\num{18938} patients; age and sex are not distributed. Five
pixel-blind models (constant predictor, slice position, volume size,
acquisition metadata, and a combination), with a within-series permutation null
as control, were fit on training slices of a subject-disjoint five-fold split
and read at slice and patient level. For each benchmark--comparator pair,
the share of the published margin over chance reached without images
(``trivial fraction'') was computed. Areas under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUCs) carry 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals
resampling subjects; no hypothesis testing was performed.

\textbf{Results:} On the hemorrhage benchmark one pixel-blind score vector read
AUC 0.737 (95\% CI: 0.735, 0.740) per slice and 0.453 (95\% CI: 0.445, 0.461)
per patient, an interval below chance; all six official labels behaved
alike (gaps, 0.205--0.307), and four independent routes agreed to 0.003. The
median trivial fraction against peer-reviewed comparators was 0.469 (IQR:
0.437, 0.490; $n=9$ arms), with one arm at $-0.002$.

\textbf{Conclusion:} A pixel-blind model reached about half of the
peer-reviewed published margin over chance at the slice level, and on the
largest benchmark fell below chance when read per patient.

\section*{Introduction}

A three-dimensional acquisition is often labeled slice by slice---this slice
contains the lesion, that one does not---and a classifier's performance is then
reported by pooling slices into a single area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (AUC). The clinical question is almost always about a
patient. The gap between them is arithmetic: a slice-level
ranking can be dominated by \emph{where} a slice sits in the stack, because
findings are not uniformly distributed along an organ, and a score vector
encoding only that geometry ranks slices well and patients not at all.

None of this is new. Shortcut learning is a named phenomenon
\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}. Badgeley et al predicted hip fracture at AUC 0.78
from the radiograph, 0.91 with hospital process features added, and
0.52---chance---on a test set balanced across those variables
\citep{badgeley2019hip}; the same mechanism has been documented across
institutions, in radiographic COVID-19 models, and across thirteen datasets
\citep{zech2018pneumonia,degrave2021covid,ongly2024shortcut}. On the evaluation
unit, Yagis et al measured slice-level cross-validation inflating test accuracy
by 29\%--55\% and reaching about 96\% on \emph{randomly labeled} data
\citep{yagis2021leakage}, Tampu et al reported the same
\citep{tampu2022leakage}, and more than half of surveyed Alzheimer disease
classification papers may have leaked
\citep{wen2020cnn,kapoor2023leakage}. Stronger baselines have been urged at
field level \citep{varoquaux2022failures,roberts2021pitfalls}.

The central construction used here is prior art. A public Kaggle notebook on the
RSNA-STR Pulmonary Embolism Detection challenge, ``Baseline with no image''
(October 2020), bins the label rate against relative slice position on the
training set and applies it to the test set with no pixels
\citep{osciiart2020baseline}. Yan et al, in the paper that defines the
DeepLesion lesion-type task, publish a ``Baseline: Location feature'' row at
59.7\% eight-class accuracy against their own method's 90.5\%
\citep{yan2018deeplesiongraphs}.

Two questions remain open. The literature on the evaluation unit measures the
cost of a \emph{wrong}, leaky split, not what survives a \emph{correct},
patient-disjoint one; and it does not quantify how much of a reported
slice-level number a pixel-free model accounts for.

The purpose of this study was to quantify how much published slice-level
performance on public volumetric imaging benchmarks is reachable from a
benchmark's own label file with no images under a subject-disjoint split, and
to test the hypothesis that the same score vector, read per patient, does not
retain that performance.

\section*{Materials and Methods}

\subsection*{Study Design}

This was a retrospective secondary analysis of publicly released, de-identified
label files from imaging benchmarks released between 2016 and 2024, together
with the numbers reported by those benchmarks' comparator papers. Analyses were
run in July 2026. No patient was recruited, no identifiable data were used, and
no pixel data were downloaded. Institutional review board approval was not
required, because no data were obtained through intervention or interaction
with any living individual and no identifiable private information was
used. Participant
age and sex are not distributed with these label files and therefore cannot be
reported.

\subsection*{Benchmark Selection}

A dataset entered the audit if the four fields a positional null needs---subject
identifier, slice index, label, and train/test assignment---could be obtained
without downloading pixel data. Seven benchmarks across eight label files
qualified: RSNA 2019 Intracranial Hemorrhage (ICH) \citep{flanders2020rsnaich},
fastMRI Prostate, T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) arms
\citep{tibrewala2023fastmriprostate}, DeepLesion \citep{yan2018deeplesion},
fastMRI+ knee \citep{zhao2022fastmriplus}, Duke Breast Cancer MRI
\citep{saha2021dukebreastmri,saha2018radiogenomics}, PI-CAI
\citep{saha2024picai}, and LUNA16 \citep{setio2017luna16}. RSNA ICH is the
largest: \num{752802} slices, \num{21744} series, \num{18938} patients.

whether machine learning can aid in the reconstruction of medical images.

\subsection*{The Zero-Image Baseline Family}

Five null models were implemented, each fit on training rows and scoring test
rows: a constant \emph{prevalence} predictor, which is the chance anchor; a
\emph{positional} model, a 20-bin estimate of the probability of the label
given relative slice position; a \emph{volume-size} model, the number of slices
in the volume; a \emph{metadata} model, a depth-limited classification tree
over acquisition and administrative columns; and a \emph{combined}
position-plus-metadata tree. Relative position is
$(\text{slice}-\text{minimum})/(\text{maximum}-\text{minimum})$ within a volume,
so volumes of different depth are comparable. The positional baseline was also
run over a 5/10/20/50 bin sweep and alongside a \emph{no-fit} variant,
$-\lvert\text{relative position}-0.5\rvert$, which uses no training data at
all; a result surviving both cannot be a binning artifact. Columns that are the
label under another name, and columns derived from the images, were excluded
from the metadata pool; every included and excluded column is written to the
run's output.

\subsection*{Evaluation, Intervals, and Nulls}

Every baseline produces one score vector per test set, read at \emph{both}
units: slice-level AUC, and patient-level AUC after aggregating each subject's
slice scores by their mean, with a maximum-aggregated reading reported
alongside. Splits were five-fold and subject-disjoint, scored out of fold and
pooled, so every subject is tested exactly once. Confidence intervals (CIs) are
percentile bootstrap over \emph{subjects}---a subject drawn twice contributes
all of that subject's slices twice---with 2000 replicates. The naive
slice-resampled interval was also computed and reported, labeled as the
incorrect one; coverage of both was measured on simulated data whose true AUC
is available in closed form (0.6880; 200 datasets of 20 patients $\times$ 15
slices). Each baseline was reported against its own permutation null; for the
positional model that null shuffles labels \emph{within each series}, which
preserves prevalence, subject clustering, and stack depth.

\subsection*{The Comparison Statistic}

\begin{equation*}
\text{trivial fraction}=\frac{\text{best zero-image baseline}-\text{chance}}
{\text{published}-\text{chance}}
\end{equation*}

with chance $=0.5$ for AUC and the majority-class rate for multiclass accuracy.
It was computed for every (benchmark, published comparator) pair. Rows are not
independent, so the primary summary takes each benchmark-arm's
\emph{strongest} published system, the conservative choice, because a stronger
comparator enlarges the denominator. Rows whose comparator had not been peer reviewed are
summarized separately and labeled as preprint comparators throughout. The
fraction is undefined when the published number is at or below chance; values
above 1 and below 0 are reported unclipped. Its interval propagates uncertainty
in the baseline only, so it is too narrow as a statement about the ratio.

\subsection*{Statistical Analysis}

This is an estimation study: no significance test was pre-specified and no $P$
values are reported. Every AUC carries a 95\% percentile bootstrap CI over 2000
replicates resampling subjects, with seeds recorded and degenerate replicates
counted rather than dropped. Analyses used Python 3.11 (Python Software
Foundation, Wilmington, Del) with NumPy 1.26 and pandas 2.1, and, for the
independent reimplementation only, scikit-learn 1.4.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{One Pixel-Blind Score Vector, Read at Two Units}

Every value here is computed from a published label file; no published number
enters. On the RSNA ICH official training file---\num{752802} slices, \num{21744} series, \num{18938} patients, the
benchmark whose own official metric is per slice---a 20-bin estimate of the
probability of the label given relative slice position, fit out of fold on a
subject-disjoint five-fold split, produces \emph{one} score vector. Read at the slice level it reaches AUC 0.737 (95\% CI:
0.735, 0.740). Read at the patient level, after mean aggregation, the same
vector reaches 0.453 (95\% CI: 0.445, 0.461)---an interval lying wholly below
chance. Aggregating instead by each patient's highest-scoring slice gives
0.500.
Nothing changes between the readings except the unit, and the difference is
0.284 AUC. All six official labels
behave the same way, with gaps of 0.205--0.307 (Table 1, Fig 1A).

Three controls ran on the same path. The within-series label permutation null
sits at 0.502 at the slice level, so the excess is 0.236; at the patient level that null is 0.523, so the observed 0.453 sits 0.069 below
what the same estimator reaches when there is nothing to find. The
constant predictor scores 0.492 per slice and 0.501 per patient. The naive
slice-resampled interval is 1.51--1.99 times
too narrow across these six rows (Fig 1B); in simulation its coverage at a
nominal 95\% was 46.5\% (93 of 200 datasets) against 91.5\% (183 of 200) for
the subject-clustered interval, with a mean width 3.18 times narrower.

Four routes agree
to 0.003 AUC at both units (Table 2), one an independent reimplementation
sharing no code with the audit tool. Three further checks come from the tool's full-cohort output: the effect is not
a binning artifact (slice AUC 0.716,
0.733, 0.738, and 0.745 over 5, 10, 20, and 50 bins, and the fit-free
centrality score reaches 0.735); the null model is not itself overfitting
(apparent training AUC 0.7379 vs held-out 0.7376); and acquisition metadata
adds nothing here (combined position-plus-metadata tree 0.718 against its own
permutation null of 0.718).

The collapse
is bin-robust at the slice level but not at the patient level, where the
full-cohort sweep runs 0.437, 0.445, 0.456, and 0.632 over 5, 10, 20, and 50
bins; at 50 bins the bin index nearly equals the raw slice index, so the
per-patient aggregate begins tracking volume length, itself weakly predictive
here (0.591 patient AUC alone). Twenty bins is the pre-specified setting
throughout.

Of the six other benchmark-arms for which both units are defined, four show the
same divergence and two do not (Table 3). DeepLesion does not collapse
and should not: its labels are anatomic regions, so position predicts them at
both units (0.977 vs 0.954 for pelvis). LUNA16 moves little at either unit and
does not diverge between them (0.534 slice, 0.581 patient). On Duke Breast a
slice-level value is computable (0.823; 95\% CI: 0.811, 0.834) and a
patient-level value is not, because all 922 of 922 patients are positive.

\subsection*{How Much of a Published Margin Is Reachable with No Pixels}

Seven benchmarks on eight label files produced 24 (benchmark, published
comparator) rows. The trivial fraction is reported as a distribution rather
than as a count of verdicts (Table 4, Fig 2).

Against peer-reviewed comparators, taking each benchmark-arm's
strongest published system, the median share of the published margin over
chance that a pixel-blind model reaches is 0.469 (IQR: 0.437, 0.490; range,
$-0.002$ to 0.613; $n=9$). The distribution is tight rather than diffuse: 8 of
those 9 rows lie between 0.30 and 0.70. The six RSNA ICH label rows are the
core of it, because they run against a peer-reviewed slice-level comparator on
the identical cohort, metric, and unit \citep{burduja2020ich}: 0.395, 0.437,
0.469, 0.490, 0.510, and 0.613 against the stronger of the two systems in that
table.

One benchmark-arm of the nine does not fire. On LUNA16, scoring the same positional estimator on
the challenge's own competition performance metric out of fold on a
scan-disjoint split gives 0.0020, and sensitivity 0.0006 at one false positive
per scan---\emph{below} a random-score reference of 0.0027, against a published
combined-solution sensitivity above 0.95, for a trivial fraction of $-0.002$
\citep{setio2017luna16}. One negative out of nine shows that the measure can
fail to fire; no claim about its false-positive rate would be supportable at
this $n$. On PI-CAI the
\emph{positional} baseline is exactly 0.500 at every bin setting, the correct
registration of ``inapplicable'' (that label file has one row per case and no
slice index) rather than a computed result; that arm is nonetheless not a null
on this statistic, because its strongest zero-image model, over acquisition
metadata, reaches 0.692 (95\% CI: 0.626, 0.755), a trivial fraction of 0.467
against a published case-level value of 0.91 \citep{saha2024picai}.
PI-CAI evaluates at the patient level by design, so fixing the reporting unit
does not by itself fix acquisition confounding.

Zero peer-reviewed comparisons are matched. Every row at or above a trivial
fraction of 1 comes from one benchmark, fastMRI Prostate, whose
comparator is an arXiv preprint carrying no journal reference and no Europe PMC
record two years after posting \citep{rempe2024kspace}. Those six rows are
reported as a worked example with a preprint comparator, not as evidence for a
general claim: on that group's own published label files, their
own patient-disjoint split, and their own evaluation unit, the zero-image
positional baseline reaches slice AUC 0.851 (95\% CI: 0.816, 0.887) against a
published headline of 0.861, a trivial fraction of 0.973 (95\% CI: 0.876,
1.073), and the same score vector read at the patient level falls to 0.424
(95\% CI: 0.298, 0.547). That is the DWI arm, which is the arm those authors
state they use; the T2-weighted arm of the same release gives 0.854 (95\% CI:
0.812, 0.891). The result does not depend on fitting anything: the no-fit
centrality score reaches 0.841 on the DWI arm. The general claim that
trivial baselines match published performance on medical imaging benchmarks is
not supported by these data, and is not supported against the peer-reviewed
literature at all.

\section*{Discussion}

The unit at which a three-dimensional benchmark is read decides what its number
means. On \num{18938} patients a single pixel-blind score vector read 0.737
(95\% CI: 0.735, 0.740) per slice and 0.453 (95\% CI: 0.445, 0.461) per patient,
on all six labels; only the first is what a paper would print. Against
peer-reviewed comparators the median share of the published margin over chance
reachable with no pixels was 0.469 (IQR: 0.437, 0.490; $n=9$), and on one arm
none of it was reachable.

A trivial fraction is a statement about an \emph{evaluation protocol}: a
pixel-blind model reaches that much of a reported margin over chance under the
same protocol. It is not a decomposition---baseline and published model may
exploit the same shortcut, different shortcuts, or overlapping ones---and it is
not a claim about any model's internals, which no analysis of a label file
could support. Reporting it continuously rather than as a verdict constrains
what may be said in both directions: it licenses the claim that a median of
about half the peer-reviewed published margin over chance is reachable with no
pixels, and it forbids the reverse, because 8 of 9 peer-reviewed rows sit near
0.5 rather than near 1.

Badgeley et al's control is a \emph{negative} one---balance the confounders and
watch the model collapse to 0.52 \citep{badgeley2019hip}. The control used here
is a \emph{positive} one: fit a model on the confounder alone and see what it
reaches, which is the form an auditor can run without the images. Against the
leakage literature the distinction is the split: those measurements are of the
inflation caused by a \emph{wrong} split, and every value here was obtained
under a correct, patient-disjoint one
\citep{yagis2021leakage,tampu2022leakage}. Against Yan et al the distinction is
the source of the position---theirs comes from a body-part regressor run on the
image, this one from a column in the published label file
\citep{yan2018deeplesiongraphs}.

For a radiologist reading an imaging AI paper, the operative question is what a
reported AUC was computed over. On the hemorrhage benchmark the slice-pooled
number stood 0.205 to 0.307 above the per-patient number computed from the same
scores, so a report that gives only the first leaves the per-patient question
unanswered, and a per-patient number is the one that corresponds to the
decision a radiologist makes.

For benchmark publishers, three fields in the label file that is already
released---a subject identifier, a slice index or $z$ position, and the official
train/test assignment---make every one of these checks runnable by anyone,
including readers who will never be granted the pixels, and publishing them
costs nothing. RSNA ICH shows what it costs not to: its slice ordering is not
in the official release and had to be recovered from a public, pixel-free
mirror, then verified by a falsifiable run-length test. What this protocol
cannot do is detect shortcuts that live in the pixels---scanner texture,
burned-in annotation, body-part framing---which need the images.

\textbf{Limitations.} The construction is prior art: a Kaggle notebook built
the pixel-free positional predictor in 2020 \citep{osciiart2020baseline} and
the paper that defines the DeepLesion lesion-type task published a
location-only baseline in 2018 \citep{yan2018deeplesiongraphs}; what is
measured here is how much published performance that construction accounts for
under a correct split, at two units, with subject-clustered statistics. No
peer-reviewed comparison is matched, and the only matched rows rest on a
preprint comparator. Two audited benchmarks are not pixel-free in the same
clean sense: fastMRI+ needs slice counts from image file headers, with coverage
of 199 of 1173 roster volumes, and LUNA16's false-positive-reduction track is
conditioned on a candidate list produced by image-based detectors, so ``zero
image'' there means ``zero image given the published candidate list.'' The
PI-CAI comparison is across cohorts, with published values on a hidden
1000-case testing cohort and the baseline on the public 1500-case development
set, so a strict reading makes those rows non-comparable; they are scored
because the caveat runs against the null. The trivial fraction's interval is
too narrow as a statement about the ratio, because it propagates uncertainty in
the baseline only. Age and sex could not be reported, because the public label
files do not carry them, so no subgroup analysis was possible. Finally, the
audit covers only benchmarks whose label fields could be obtained without a
data-use agreement covering pixels.

In summary, the unit at which a three-dimensional benchmark is read decides
what its number means: the same pixel-blind score vector ranked slices well
above chance and patients at or below it, and reached a median of about half
the peer-reviewed published margin over chance. Three fields in the label file
a benchmark already publishes make all of that checkable by anyone, without the
images.
